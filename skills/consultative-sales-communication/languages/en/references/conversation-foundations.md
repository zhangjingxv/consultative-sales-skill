# Conversation foundations: listening, responding and shared understanding

Use when replies sound like questionnaires, scripts or lectures, or the customer says they have been misunderstood. This expands M03, M05, M06, M19 and M22 without adding tactics. These are revisable communication judgments, not mind-reading or evidence of sales effectiveness.

## Identify what this turn is doing

The same topic may be a shared experience, direct question, expression of feeling or boundary. Follow explicit wording, not personality labels; do not expose internal classifications to the customer.

- Sharing: respond to one real detail; add a truthful observation if useful. No question or advice is required.
- Question: answer first when possible; clarify only facts that determine the answer. Do not evade a direct question with discovery.
- Feeling: acknowledge the stated impact. Do not automatically prescribe a solution. Ask whether listening or problem-solving would help only when this is genuinely unclear and changes the action, not every turn.
- Boundary: narrow or stop the action as stated. “No advice” is not a request for subtler advice.

Acknowledging feelings does not validate unverified accusations. Existing faults, complaints and delivery obligations require action alongside understanding; listening is not an excuse to neglect a duty.

## Let new information change your next sentence

Select a concrete detail the customer actually expressed and respond to its relevance, rather than saying only “understood” or “exactly.” Briefly synthesize multiple conditions, ambiguity or a consequential decision and leave room for correction. Do not echo every answer.

When corrected: acknowledge the mistaken judgment, replace the old assumption with the new information, and change the action. Do not re-ask what the customer has explained. Keep requested records only in the user's designated private location; never claim persistent storage that has not happened.

Failure: the customer says price is not the issue, yet the seller offers a discount. Saying “I understand your support concern” while continuing to sell on price still fails to update understanding.

## Both people contribute; it is not an interview

Do not force acknowledgment, contribution and question into every turn. When useful, offer one truthful, relevant observation, personal experience, verification method or action within the user's responsibility. Otherwise stay brief; do not invent shared experiences to create closeness.

Before expanding a story or explanation, establish how it serves the current topic. A direct question or volunteered follow-up may justify detail; polite praise does not. Stop when the answer is sufficient. Do not take over the conversation or make help conditional on budget information, private disclosures or orders.

A topic change needs a clear connection to a concrete point already expressed. Everyday sharing can end without a business pivot. Do not divert from unresolved objections, complaints or factual unknowns. There is no need to ask “want to hear more?” every time.

## Appreciate specific actions, not imagined character

Mention only observable conduct and its actual benefit. When the effect is unknown, acknowledge the action without inventing results. Match the user's ordinary voice; use neither praise quotas nor implied repayment.

If the customer marked the edits: “Thanks for highlighting the changes; I can check them one by one.”
Not: “You're always the technical expert, so leave this purchase to us.” Do not invent cost savings from the action.

## Explain in familiar terms without replacing evidence

Start with a plain-language answer. Add one relatable analogy if needed, then return to precise conditions, unknowns and verification. Neither jargon nor simplification should conceal limitations.

Analogies explain concepts; they do not establish compatibility, performance gains or investment returns. Clarify ambiguous customer terminology rather than adopting an incorrect technical conclusion.

## Original synthetic exchanges: did information change the reply?

### Wants to talk, not a plan

Customer: “Several rounds of discussions have worn me out today. No solutions for now; I just want to vent.” No unresolved service duty exists.
Draft: “That sounds draining after so many discussions. Go ahead—I'm listening.”
Do not add productivity advice, diagnose causes or pitch a product. Move to problem-solving if the customer subsequently asks for help.

### Reciprocal sharing without a required question

Customer: “Finished my first lap around the lake on the bike this weekend. Quite windy.” The user explicitly said they rode that route and found the headwind hard going.
Draft: “Well done on that first lap! I found that headwind stretch tough when I rode it too.”
Without the supplied experience, never invent “me too”; simply respond to the first completion and the wind.

### Correction changes the action

Earlier seller: “Is the purchasing budget still undecided?”
Customer: “No, funding is approved. I don't know who to contact if something fails after launch.”
Draft: “I misunderstood. The open issue is responsibility after launch. I'll check the support scope and contact person, and mark anything still unconfirmed.”
The user must be able to perform that check. Do not re-ask about budget or promise unverified service coverage.

### Care alongside responsibility

Customer: “It's down again. I really can't explain it a third time.” This is the same fault the user is responsible for following up. They can continue coordinating, but the cause and repair time remain unconfirmed.
Draft: “Another interruption, and having to explain it again, is disruptive. I'll use the records from the first two reports to continue the investigation and consolidate any essential gaps.”
Do not request known facts again, replace action with comfort, or guarantee an immediate fix.

These are drafts with prerequisites to check, not evidence of messages sent, actions completed or improved relationships.
